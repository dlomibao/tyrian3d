Art remastering and 3D modelling

How a 2D sprite from Tyrian becomes a 3D model in the game, and how each model is checked.

Sprite (game data)→References→Concept image→ Feature list→Blender script→Review sheet→ Grade → fix → regrade→Human check→PR, CI, merge

Noteworthy: problems we hit and how we solved them

The most interesting part of the remake is that most models were made and improved by AI agents, and the hard problems were about judging 3D work from 2D pixel art reliably. In roughly the order we ran into them:

ProblemWhat we did
An AI grader can't be trusted on its own. Can a model judge 3D models from renders the way a person would? A fixed, one-page review sheet for every model, and a local rating page where a person scores sheets with pins on problem spots. A committee (an Opus grader, a Fable grader and a chair) pre-filled 20 ratings and the person checked them; they agreed with all of them, and the two models agreed within 1 point on 124 of 128 scores. Those ratings became the calibration set the rubric refers to.
The ships were graded against the wrong picture. The sheet tool took a ship's first frame, which is its hardest left bank, so a level model was compared with a tilted sprite. Found because the USP Talon's sheet looked shifted. The tool now uses each model's own frame, and the two affected ratings were marked superseded and regraded.
Automated fixes made models taller but wrong. Agents read the sprite's drawn lighting literally: highlights became lumps, the sprite's black outline and shading were painted on, and in game many models turned nearly black. Two changes. Rules in the rubric: highlights are shine, the outline and shading are lighting, judge the material first. And a game-lit panel: the sheets had used Blender's flat Workbench renderer, which ignores metalness, so no grader could see shine. Panel 9 now renders with the materials' metalness and roughness under game-like light. A lighting pass then fixed all 87 reworked models.
Features disappeared. The Gencore Phoenix lost its windshield and went bubbly; the blue-pipe machine's pipes became a row of tanks. Each round was only compared with the previous round, so small losses added up. Feature lists (named parts with boxes on the sprite), comparison with the pre-loop baseline (the hand-made models, rebuilt from an old commit), and a close-up tile per feature: sprite | baseline | now. Any weakened or lost feature reverts the change.
What is that cluster of pixels? A 3-pixel glint might be a cockpit, a bolt or a light. Concept images from Gemini, made with the sprite and a prompt that keeps its outline. Comparing many of them with their sprites gave general sprite-reading rules (bright line = edge, streak = cylinder, bands = rings, saturated colour with a white core = light, dark slot plus glint = glass).
Rocks turned into blobs. We first blamed the shape. The real cause: one flat matte colour under the game's mostly overhead light makes small facets all the same brightness. Bigger, steeper facets that face the sun, in the rock's mid-tone colour, so the shape reads under the same lighting.
Pipes that bend. Two attempts to bend the pipes into the heart-shaped loop left a flange or a capsule end. Build each pipe and the loop as one tube swept along one curve, so the thickness can't change at the joint.
Getting images into Gemini from a script. Pasting image data through the agent occasionally corrupted it (blank thumbnails, failed requests); wide strips were refused; Chrome blocked repeated downloads; tall results were missed by a size check. Send the sprite as a small palette plus one character per pixel, checked and drawn on a canvas in the page; or put the PNG on the clipboard and press ⌘V. Wide strips are cropped. The person allowed automatic downloads, and results are picked by their largest side.
Agents and interruptions. A usage limit cut agents off mid-edit; agents spawned on a model the person had stopped using declined and returned placeholder scores of 1. Every fix backs up its files first, so half-done edits are restored before resuming. Results are screened for placeholder grades, and those models are reverted and redone.

Older decisions are worth a look too. Draws are lifted along camera rays, so the 3D screen layout is exactly the original's (D6). Meshes store palette indices and take their colour from a palette texture, so fades and the hit flash still work (D7). Models animate with their sprites through frame-tagged parts and colours (D17).

The goal

Every enemy, boss, structure and player ship is a Blender model that replaces its sprite (D15). A model must be the sprite brought into 3D: the same outline from above, the same proportions, colours and details, with believable depth. The game camera looks almost straight down, so the top view must match the sprite closely; depth shows in the lighting, the shadows and the slight tilt. Models are low-poly so a screen full of enemies stays fast.

The original game data (tyrian21.zip) is never committed or deployed (D3). Everything we make from it, such as models, concept images and redrawn pictures, may be public, credited to the original artists in CREDITS.md (D16, D41).

1. References from the game data

These are generated locally from your own copy of the data and are git-ignored:

npm run catalogue        # plays every level headless; writes reference/models/ (about 2 min)
  • reference/models/catalogue.json lists every model to make, in priority order: player ships, then flying enemies, then ground enemies, each by time on screen. Each entry lists the levels it appears in (1.3 = episode 1, level file 3, the first level, TYRIAN).
  • reference/models/<name>/ holds sprite.json (every frame's palette indices and colours), frame PNGs, a contact sheet and, for a piece of a bigger object, assembly.png. Bosses and big ground structures are assemblies: several 2×2 enemies side by side, each with its own model, built together so the pieces fit.
  • Sprite sheets (reference/sprites/) and the background tiles and maps (reference/tiles/, from tests/tools/tileReference.test.ts) cover pickups, shots and buildings.

2. Concept images

A high-resolution reimagining of the sprite settles what each cluster of pixels is (a canopy, a pipe, a clamp, a light) before anyone models it. They're made with Google Gemini (Nano Banana), at gemini.google.com/images, with the sprite enlarged with nearest-neighbour scaling and this prompt:

a high def enhancement of this ship from tyrian the game that keeps the same overhead profile a la starcraft remastered. don't take the sprite pixel graphics literally. you shouldn't make pixel shaped details in the high def version unless that's what the image reads as. it must fit in the same outline as original for collision detection purposes
  • Swap "ship" for "enemy ship", "ground structure (seen from overhead)" or "ground building" as needed. For sets of small items (pickups, shots), arrange them in a roughly square grid and ask Gemini to keep each item in its place.
  • Long thin strips (fences, walls) and very wide images tend to fail or come back as unrelated pipes or blades. Crop or split them. Gemini sometimes declines a request; trying again or re-padding the image usually works.
  • Bigger images are best pasted from the clipboard (copy the PNG, ⌘V into the prompt box). For automation, pass the sprite as a palette plus a grid of characters and draw it on a canvas in the page; that never corrupts the image.
  • Save to assets/blender/features/concepts/<name>.jpg (at most 1024 px) and add a row to concepts/index.json. Mark a concept "note": "Unreliable…" if Gemini misread the sprite; fixers then ignore it.

A concept is a guide to what the parts are and their shape language. The sprite still decides the top-view outline, the proportions and the colours.

3. Feature lists

Each model has assets/blender/features/<name>.json: the parts a player would recognise, each with a box on the sprite, checkable traits and a material. A lost or weakened feature counts as a regression, whatever else improved.

{"name": "ship-gencore-phoenix",
 "summary": "Grey angular hard-surface fighter …",
 "concept": {"image": "assets/blender/features/concepts/ship-gencore-phoenix.jpg", "use": "design guide …"},
 "features": [
  {"id": "windshield", "kind": "glass", "box": [9, 6, 15, 10],
   "traits": ["glossy dark glass, crisp edges, set in a frame", "bright glint is shine, not a bump"],
   "note": "The cockpit …"}]}

Write them from the sprite, the concept, and the original hand-made model's script (its docstring names the parts). For player ships, the person checks the list before models change.

4. Building a model

Each model is built by a Python script run in Blender; the script is the source of truth (assets/blender/README.md).

  • Enemies are in assets/blender/enemies/<name>.py, ships in assets/blender/ships/ and the USP Talon in assets/blender/usp_talon.py. Shared helpers are in common.py and per-sheet lib_bNNN.py.
  • One sprite pixel is 1/12 unit, forward is +Y, up is +Z, and the model is centred on its sprite block.
  • Palette materials (pal_<index>_<tag>) take their colour from the live palette, so level palettes, fades and the hit flash still work (D7). Each material's metalness and roughness are kept in game, and the scene has environment light, so polished metal shines.
  • Animation (D17): common.frame_part shows a part only on some frames, and a palette material can change colour per frame.
  • Assemblies are built once in assembly coordinates and cut into their pieces with common.split_assembly.
tools/build-models.sh assets/blender/enemies/newsh2-127.py   # writes public/models/… .glb and src/render/spriteModels/… .json
npm run dev                                                   # then ?viewer=newsh2-127 to inspect it (&height=N tries other heights)

Rules that came out of review

tools/calibration/rubric.md has them all. The ones that matter most:

  • Read the sprite, don't copy it. A thin bright line is an edge catching the light. A bright streak along a long part means a cylinder. Light and dark bands across a part are rings. A grid of squares is bolted plating. A saturated colour with a white core is a light. A dark slot with one bright pixel is glass. Dark areas inside the outline are depth.
  • The sprite's lighting is not shape or paint. A small hot spot on metal is shine, not a lump. Don't paint the sprite's dark outline or drawn shading onto the model: the game draws its own outline and lights the model, so painted shading darkens it twice.
  • Judge the material first. Metal and glass are shiny. On rock, earth and organic parts a highlight is a lit face of real shape.
  • Hard surfaces stay hard, round parts stay round. Ships and machines are flat panels with crisp edges, plus the round parts the sprite draws (canopies, nacelles, pods, domes).
  • Pipes are pipes: constant thickness along a path, bends included, never a chain of bulges. Glass stays glass. Keep every feature.

5. Verification

Review sheets

python tools/review_sheet.py <name> --which current --baseline --out DIR

This renders one image (with metrics in a .json beside it), always in the same layout:

PanelShows
1The sprite, enlarged with nearest-neighbour scaling
2, 3, 4The model from straight above at the sprite's scale; the sprite laid over it; a diff (missing pixels, extra pixels, colour error)
5, 6Side and front elevations with a ruler in sprite pixels
7, 8Three-quarter views
9The game camera's angle, lit like the game (EEVEE, the materials' metalness and roughness). The only panel that shows shine.
10, 11, 12Region map, inferred heights (a hint only) and metrics: outline match (IoU), colour error, height, triangles
13–15With --baseline: the hand-made model from before any automated changes (a checkout of commit 7d47c05)
Feature tilesOne per listed feature: sprite | baseline | now, cropped from game-lit top views

Review sheets are made from the game data, so they stay local.

Calibrating the grader

Before letting agents grade, a person rated 20 sheets on the local rating page (python tools/calibration/server.py --sheets DIR, at http://127.0.0.1:5194/). An Opus and a Fable grader plus a chair pre-filled the drafts, and the person agreed with all of them. Those ratings (assets/blender/proto/calibration/feedback.json) are the reference the rubric points to. Opus and Fable agreed within 1 point on 124 of 128 scores, so one grader per check is enough.

The grade → fix → regrade loop (D39)

  1. Fix. An agent reads the rubric, the feature list, the concept and the latest grade, backs up the files it will touch, edits only that model's own script, rebuilds, renders a new sheet, and iterates.
  2. Regrade. A separate grader, seeing only the before and after sheets (never the fixer's reasoning), scores the new sheet and rates each listed feature against the baseline: better, same, weakened or lost.
  3. Keep or revert. If anything is worse, the backups are restored. Otherwise the change is kept. A model gets at most 3 rounds and stops at 4/5 with no high-severity issues, or when a round changes nothing.

Fixers must not edit shared lib_*.py, common.py, tools or game code, nor change model names, frames, animation, palette naming or the origin. They may add at most about +60% triangles. These runs use the Workflow tool; the person sets the model, effort and concurrency (lately at most 3 Opus agents at medium effort, no Fable).

Before merging

  • Look at a before-and-after contact sheet of every changed model (sprite | before | after, game view). Graders miss things a person sees, such as everything turning dark, highlights built as lumps, lost windshields or pipes becoming tanks.
  • Check that no shared library, common.py or src/render/spriteModels/ entry changed unexpectedly. Rebuild every changed script and confirm the changed .glb set is the same. A few scripts write slightly different bytes on each run; that's harmless.
  • Run npx vitest run (including the model coverage test) and npx tsc --noEmit. Merge only after CI passes.
  • For player ships, and anything the person flagged, send them images and wait for approval.
